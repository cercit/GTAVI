Shark Cards

Real money in, GTA$ out. Five cards, from $4.99 to $99.99 in US stores. They're the reason GTA Online's updates could stay free for 13 years, and the part of it that gets criticised most.

The GTA+ member

Already pays monthly. GTA+ members get 15% more GTA$ on the same card at the same price: a Great White gives GTA$1,725,000 instead of GTA$1,500,000.

Account 04

The player who never pays

Not the customer, but the most important one to protect. If the game stops being playable for them, the card stops being a shortcut and starts feeling like a toll.

02

The problem it solved

For players: "I want it now, not after twenty more missions." A card fills your in-game bank. It doesn't raise your level or unlock anything that needs a level. It only buys time.

For Rockstar: how does a game sold once in 2013 keep paying for new content? Rockstar said in 2014 that Shark Cards let it keep updates free and the community in one piece. No paid map packs splitting players into those who bought and those who didn't. You buy them on the store of whatever platform you play on, including Rockstar's own launcher.

Free updates for everyone, paid for by the people who don't want to wait.

03

How it makes money

Directly, every card is a sale: you pay the store, Rockstar drops a fixed amount of GTA$ into your account. Take-Two files it under "recurrent consumer spending", alongside add-ons and other in-game purchases, and has never said how much comes from Shark Cards alone.

The clever part is the price ladder. Look at how much GTA$ one real dollar buys on each card.

Worked out from Rockstar's GTA$ amounts and US store prices. The bigger the card, the more each dollar buys: the $99.99 Megalodon gives twice as much per dollar as the $4.99 Tiger.

06

What I'd change

The card isn't the problem. The gap is. Over the years new cars and businesses got more and more expensive in GTA$, so the distance between "play for it" and "pay for it" kept growing. Eurogamer wrote in 2022 that new players meet a Shark Card pop-up almost everywhere they turn. Rockstar's December 2022 fix raised what cards give; it didn't make the game itself pay better.

As a PM, I'd do three things. Show the real-money price next to anything priced in GTA$, which is what EU consumer authorities asked games to do in 2025. Limit how often a brand-new player sees the card offer in their first week. And set, and publish, a target for how many hours a typical new car should take to earn by playing, so the shortcut stays a shortcut.

Monetisation and pricing psychology

A price ladder makes each step up look like better value. Here, the $99.99 card gives twice as much GTA$ per dollar as the $4.99 one, so bigger feels smarter.

Anchoring works the other way: once you've seen a $99.99 card, $19.99 feels small. And selling time is the real product: the card doesn't sell a car, it sells the hours you'd have spent earning it.

The question for any PM: where is the line between monetisation and greed? When paying stops being a shortcut and becomes the only realistic road. A shortcut is a product; a toll is a problem.
